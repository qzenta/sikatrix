Over the past few weeks, a few of our clients have asked some version of the same question: "Why did SARS just register my business for VAT? I never applied."

It's a fair thing to be alarmed by. But it isn't a scam, and it isn't a SARS error. It's called automatic VAT registration, and it's something SARS has always had the legal power to do — it's just become more visible recently, for a few concrete reasons we'll unpack below.

## What Is Automatic VAT Registration?

Under the VAT Act, any business making taxable supplies above the compulsory threshold in any rolling 12-month period is legally required to apply for VAT registration within 21 business days of crossing it. Automatic registration is what happens when a business doesn't do this — either because it didn't realise it had crossed the threshold, or simply never got around to applying.

SARS's own published guidance confirms that if its data — drawn from banks, CIPC company records, and other third-party submissions — shows a business's taxable supplies exceed the compulsory threshold, SARS may register that business as a VAT vendor without a formal application ever being submitted. The business is then notified and must link the VAT tax type on eFiling and begin filing returns from the effective date SARS sets.

## Why Is This Happening More Now?

Two things changed recently that explain the recent wave of surprise:

1. The thresholds moved in the 2026 Budget. Effective 1 April 2026, the compulsory VAT registration threshold rose from R1 million to R2.3 million in taxable supplies over any 12-month period, and the voluntary threshold rose from R50,000 to R120,000. Many businesses that were comfortably below the old R1 million mark — and never gave VAT much thought — are now being assessed against a different number, and some are discovering their turnover already crossed it before they'd registered.

2. SARS has been modernising its registration and data-matching systems. In December 2025, SARS announced enhancements to the VAT registration process aimed at giving applicants clearer, more specific communication. This sits alongside SARS's broader, ongoing push to draw more consistently on third-party data — from banks, financial institutions, and other mandated data providers — to verify what businesses report. The practical effect is that mismatches between a business's actual turnover and its VAT status get picked up faster and more often than before.

None of this is a new law or a new crackdown campaign — it's the same compulsory-registration rule that has always existed, applied against updated numbers with better visibility into who's crossed them.

## What to Do If SARS Registers You Automatically

If you receive a notice like this, here's the practical sequence:

1. Don't ignore it. The registration is legally effective from the date SARS sets, whether or not you've logged into eFiling yet.
2. Check the effective date carefully. SARS can backdate registration to when your turnover first crossed the threshold — which can mean VAT, penalties, and interest are owed for periods where you never charged VAT to your customers. This is the part that catches people out financially, not the registration itself.
3. Link the VAT tax type on eFiling and confirm your details are correct before your first return falls due.
4. Get a second opinion on the effective date and any backdated liability before you accept it at face value. Effective dates and backdating are exactly the kind of thing worth having a professional check — there can be room to engage with SARS on the specifics, especially if the data used doesn't reflect your actual position.

## When the Effective Date Reaches Further Back Than You Expect

Some of the notices we've seen set an effective date well over a year in the past — not just from last month, but from a specific date back in a prior tax year. This raises two practical problems at once: how do you file returns going all the way back, and what if you no longer have the invoices to support them?

A few things worth knowing:

- SARS's own eFiling system only allows self-service backdating up to 6 months. If the effective date SARS has set goes back further than that, correcting or contesting it isn't something that can be sorted out purely online — it typically requires an in-person SARS branch visit with supporting documentation, not just a portal request.
- The absence of invoices doesn't cancel the liability, but it does change what you can claim. Even without a full invoice trail, SARS still expects output VAT to be calculated on total taxable sales for the backdated period, worked out using the 15/115 tax fraction from whatever sales records exist (bank statements, sales ledgers, contracts) — you're deemed to owe VAT on those sales whether or not you actually charged customers for it at the time. The bigger cost of missing invoices is usually on the other side of the ledger: input VAT claims on business expenses require a valid tax invoice, so gaps in your own purchase records can mean genuinely losing the ability to claim back VAT you did pay, not just an administrative headache.
- If you don't think the date is actually correct, that's worth challenging, not just accepting. SARS is meant to be backdating to the point where your turnover genuinely crossed the threshold — not an arbitrary date. Ask SARS directly (or have your practitioner ask) what data they used to set that specific date, and check it against your own records. If the underlying data doesn't hold up, there's a real basis to have the date reviewed and corrected, rather than treating it as final.
- Where the liability is genuine but the records are incomplete, get a practitioner to reconstruct the period properly — from bank statements and whatever documentation does exist — rather than guessing or ignoring the gap. An incomplete but honest reconstruction, done properly and submitted, puts you in a materially better position with SARS than silence.

This is exactly the kind of situation where DIY eFiling gets risky fast — the mechanics of backdated returns, and the difference between what you owe and what you can actually prove, is worth a professional set of eyes before you file anything.
