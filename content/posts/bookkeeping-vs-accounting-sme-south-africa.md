We get some version of this question most weeks: "I already do my own books on a spreadsheet — why do I need an accountant too?" It's a reasonable question, and the honest answer is that bookkeeping and accounting are two different functions doing two different jobs. Most SMEs need both, just not necessarily at the same intensity, and confusing the two is one of the quieter ways a growing business loses control of its numbers.

This article sets out what each function actually does, where the line between them sits, and how to work out what your business needs right now versus what it will need in twelve months.

## Bookkeeping: The Record of What Happened

Bookkeeping is the ongoing, transactional recording of every financial event in your business — every sale, every expense, every payment in or out, every bank transaction. It is a record-keeping function. Done properly, it answers one question reliably at any point in time: what actually happened, and where did the money go?

A bookkeeper's core responsibilities typically include:

- Capturing and categorising every transaction (sales, purchases, expenses)
- Bank reconciliations — matching your bank statement to your books, transaction by transaction
- Managing accounts receivable (money owed to you) and accounts payable (money you owe)
- Processing supplier invoices and customer invoices
- Maintaining a general ledger that is accurate and current
- Producing basic monthly reports: a trial balance, an income and expense summary, an aged debtors/creditors list

Bookkeeping is inherently backward-looking and detail-heavy. It is also the foundation everything else is built on. If the books are wrong or incomplete, every report, tax return, and financial statement built from them will be wrong too — no accountant can produce accurate financial statements from bad bookkeeping.

## Accounting: What the Numbers Mean and What to Do About Them

Accounting takes the bookkeeper's output and turns it into something you can make decisions with, submit to SARS, or hand to a bank. It is a higher-level, interpretive function.

An accountant's work typically includes:

- Preparing Annual Financial Statements (AFS) to the applicable reporting framework (IFRS for SMEs, for most private companies)
- Reviewing and correcting the bookkeeping data for accuracy before anything is finalised
- Income tax, provisional tax, and VAT return preparation and submission
- Financial analysis — margins, cash flow trends, cost structure — and what they mean for the business
- Tax planning to legally minimise liability
- Advising on entity structure, financing decisions, and compliance obligations
- Signing off financial statements as a registered professional where third parties (banks, funders, CIPC, SARS) require it

Where bookkeeping asks "what happened," accounting asks "what does this mean, and what do we do next." An accountant is also, in most cases, the person whose signature makes a set of financial statements or a tax submission credible to a third party — a spreadsheet you compiled yourself, however accurate, does not carry that.

## The Line Isn't Always Where People Think It Is

A few misconceptions we run into regularly:

**"My accountant does my bookkeeping too, so it's the same thing."** Many accounting firms, including ours, do offer bookkeeping as part of a bundled service — but that's a business decision about what services to offer under one roof, not evidence that the two functions are the same skill. A firm doing both is still doing two distinct jobs for you.

**"I don't need a bookkeeper if I'm not VAT registered."** VAT registration is irrelevant to whether you need clean books. Any business that wants to know its actual profitability, needs financial statements for a loan application, or simply wants to stop guessing where the money went needs reliable bookkeeping regardless of VAT status.

**"Once a year, my accountant sorts everything out from my bank statements."** This works until it doesn't — usually right when you need a tax clearance certificate urgently, or a bank wants management accounts for a loan application, and a year of reconstruction stands between you and either. Reconstructing twelve months of bookkeeping from bank statements alone is also slower, more expensive, and less accurate than keeping current records, because context that would have been obvious in the moment (why was this transfer made, was this expense business or personal) is often lost by the time someone reconstructs it months later.

## What This Actually Costs You If You Get It Wrong

The businesses we see run into trouble aren't usually the ones doing no bookkeeping at all — those at least know they have a gap. The trouble tends to come from businesses that think their DIY bookkeeping is "good enough," right up until:

- A bank or funder asks for management accounts and the numbers don't reconcile to the bank statement
- SARS raises a query and there's no clean audit trail to answer it with
- The business owner discovers, at tax season, that a full year of categorisation errors has to be unwound before a return can even be filed
- A CIPC annual return or AFS deadline arrives and the underlying records aren't ready to build statements from

Every one of these is more expensive to fix after the fact than it would have been to do correctly from the start — both in accountant fees for the clean-up work and in the compliance risk sitting there in the meantime.

## Which One Does Your Business Actually Need Right Now?

A rough guide, though every business is different:

**You need bookkeeping if:** you have any regular volume of transactions (broadly, more than about 15 a month), you're not confident your bank balance and your books agree, or you're spending your own time on data entry instead of running the business.

**You need accounting on top of that if:** you're a registered company with CIPC filing obligations, you're applying for finance or a loan, you have provisional tax or VAT obligations, or you simply want a professional opinion on what your numbers mean rather than just a record of what they are.

**Most growing SMEs need both, continuously** — monthly bookkeeping to keep the records current and accurate, with an accountant reviewing that work, filing the required returns, and preparing the annual financial statements at year-end. That combination is what keeps a business audit-ready, loan-ready, and SARS-compliant year-round, instead of scrambling once a year.
