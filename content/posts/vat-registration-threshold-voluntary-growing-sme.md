Most conversations about VAT registration treat it as something that happens to you — you cross a threshold, SARS notices, you register. That's true for compulsory registration. What gets overlooked is that voluntary registration is also available well before you hit that threshold, and for the right business, registering early can genuinely work in your favour rather than being an administrative burden you're avoiding for as long as possible.

This article sets out the current thresholds, how voluntary registration actually works, and the practical trade-offs to weigh before deciding either way.

## The Current Thresholds

Following the 2026 Budget, the VAT registration thresholds changed effective 1 April 2026:

- **Compulsory registration threshold:** R2.3 million in taxable supplies over any rolling 12-month period (up from R1 million)
- **Voluntary registration threshold:** R120,000 in taxable supplies over any rolling 12-month period (up from R50,000)

Compulsory registration is not optional once you cross R2.3 million — you're legally required to apply within 21 business days of crossing the threshold, and SARS can and does register businesses automatically if you don't. Voluntary registration, by contrast, is available to any business with taxable supplies above R120,000 that chooses to register before being forced to.

## Why Would You Register Before You Have To?

It seems counterintuitive to take on VAT compliance obligations — output tax, VAT201 returns, invoicing requirements — before you're legally required to. But there are real, practical reasons SMEs choose to register early:

**You can claim input VAT on your own expenses.** If your business has significant VAT-inclusive costs — equipment, stock, professional services, rent — voluntary registration lets you claim that input VAT back rather than absorbing it as a cost. For a capital-intensive start-up phase, this can be a meaningful cash flow benefit.

**Some clients and suppliers only work with VAT-registered businesses.** Larger corporate clients and government tenders in particular often require suppliers to be VAT registered, sometimes as an explicit condition of doing business. If your growth strategy depends on landing bigger clients, registering voluntarily can remove a barrier before it costs you a contract.

**It signals a certain scale.** Rightly or wrongly, being VAT registered is sometimes read by clients, banks, and suppliers as a marker that a business is established and compliant, not a side hustle. This isn't a reason to register on its own, but it's a real factor for some businesses weighing the decision.

**You avoid a disruptive backdated registration later.** If your turnover is on a clear growth trajectory and you're going to cross R2.3 million within the next year or two regardless, registering on your own terms — with clean systems and pricing already built around VAT — is considerably less disruptive than being automatically registered later with a backdated effective date and a scramble to reconstruct records and adjust pricing retroactively. We've covered what that scramble looks like in practice in [our article on SARS's automatic VAT registration](/resources/sars-automatic-vat-registration-2026).

## The Case Against Registering Early

Voluntary registration isn't automatically the right call. The trade-offs run the other way too:

**You have to charge VAT on everything you sell.** If your customers are mostly individual consumers or small businesses who aren't VAT registered themselves, adding 15% to your prices (or absorbing it into your margin) is a real competitive disadvantage with no offsetting input VAT benefit on their side.

**Compliance overhead starts immediately.** VAT201 returns, correct tax invoices, and accurate record-keeping become non-negotiable the moment you register — SARS can disallow input claims and impose penalties for non-compliant invoicing regardless of how small your turnover still is.

**It's harder to reverse than to start.** De-registering from VAT is possible but involves its own process and SARS scrutiny, and reversing pricing decisions built around VAT-inclusive pricing is awkward with existing customers. Registering is a decision worth thinking through properly, not something to do provisionally.

## How to Actually Decide

The right call comes down to a few concrete questions specific to your business:

1. **Who are your customers?** If they are predominantly other VAT-registered businesses, voluntary registration is usually a low-cost decision — they can claim the input VAT you charge them, so your effective prices to them don't change. If they are mostly individual consumers, the calculation is different.

2. **What does your input VAT look like?** Run the numbers on a typical month or quarter. If you're carrying significant VAT-inclusive costs relative to your revenue, the input VAT claim may outweigh the compliance burden.

3. **How close are you to R2.3 million, and how fast are you growing?** If you're going to cross the compulsory threshold within the next 12–18 months regardless, there's a reasonable argument for registering on your own terms now rather than being caught by an automatic registration with a backdated effective date later.

4. **Are you compliance-ready?** Voluntary registration only makes sense if your bookkeeping and invoicing can actually support it from day one. Registering voluntarily with weak records creates SARS query risk without any of the upside.

This is not a decision to make from a rule of thumb — it needs an actual look at your numbers, your customer base, and your growth trajectory.
